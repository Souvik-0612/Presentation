\section{Introduction}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{TG-158: non-target doses}
  The report aims to address the following,
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Highlight major concerns of nontarget dose
    \item Provide a rough estimate of doses associated with different treatment approaches in clinical practice
    \item Discuss the uses of dosimeters and phantoms for measuring photon, electron, and neutron exposures
    \item Discuss the use of calculation techniques (including  Monte Carlo) for dosimetric evaluations
    \item Highlight techniques that may be considered for reducing nontarget doses
    \item Discuss dose reporting
    \item Make recommendations for clinical and research practice
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{What is outfield dosimetry}
  \begin{itemize}
    \justifying
    \item Improved conformality has not mitigated the problem of doses to normal tissues outside the treated volume. With modern treatment planning systems, high-dose regions and areas within the primary-beam path are typically well described.
    \item However, the accuracy of dose determinations beyond a few centimeters outside the treatment field edge is usually poor. \cite{huang_accuracy_2013}
    \item The dose outside the PTV is referred to as \textcolor{red}{“nontarget dose”}.
    \item Nontarget dose is delivered by nontarget radiation and can be subdivided into two categories
    \begin{table}
    \begin{tabular}{p{2cm}|p{8cm}}
      \hline\hline
      Term & Definition \\
      \hline 
      In-field & within a \textcolor{blue}{primary field border}, such as entrance and exit dose along the beam path. \\
      \hline
      Out-of-field & not only \textcolor{blue}{outside of the PTV but also outside of any primary field edge} — dose deposited by stray, or secondary, radiation. \\
      \hline\hline
    \end{tabular}
    \end{table}
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

  \begin{itemize}
    \item Nontarget dose can be classified as one of three approximate dose levels \cite{kry_span_2017}.
    \begin{tabular}{p{2cm}|p{8cm}}
      \hline\hline
      Dose & Definition \\
      \hline 
      High doses & \textcolor{blue}{($>$ 30 Gy or $>$ 50\% of the prescription dose)} are typically directly optimized during the planning process. \\
      \hline
      Intermediate doses & \textcolor{blue}{(3 - 30 Gy or 5-50\% of the prescription dose)} are also often addressed during optimization but are not generally the focus of the treatment plan \\
      \hline
      Low doses & \textcolor{blue}{($<$ 3 Gy or $<$ 5\% of the prescription dose)} are typically not considered during optimization and are often poorly modeled by the TPS. \\
      \hline\hline
    \end{tabular}
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Sources and description of out-of-field dose}
  \framesubtitle{Neutron production}
      \begin{itemize}
        \justifying
        \item When beam energies exceed 10 MV, neutron production in the head of the accelerator becomes relevant.
        \item The jaws or multileaf collimators (MLC) can also be a major source of neutrons when they intersect the primary photon beam (such as for a closed field).
        \item Neutron production in the patient is negligible because of the \textcolor{blue}{low ($\lambda$,n) cross section} for the low-atomic number materials that constitute tissue.
        \item Neutron production in the accelerator head results in \textcolor{cyan}{isotropic} neutron emissions, exposing the patient to a relatively uniform bath of neutrons. The fluence of neutrons and associated \textcolor{cyan}{dose equivalents increase linearly with the number of MU delivered}.
	\end{itemize}    
\end{frame}




\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Sources and description of out-of-field dose}
  \framesubtitle{Neutron production}
   \begin{columns}
    \begin{column}{0.55\textwidth}
      \begin{itemize}
        \justifying
        \item Secondary neutron spectra measured in air in the patient plane are shown and include a fast neutron peak centered between \textcolor{teal}{0.1 and 1 MeV}, a maximum energy of approximately 10 MeV, and a low-energy tail that arises from neutrons being elastically scattered throughout the treatment vault.
        \item Varian accelerators generate approximately twice as many neutrons as Siemens or Elekta accelerators because they \textcolor{red}{have a higher peak photon energy} for the same nominal beam energy.
	\end{itemize}

\begin{frame}
  \frametitle{Sources and description of out-of-field dose  }
  \framesubtitle{Neutron dose deposition}
   \begin{columns}
    \begin{column}{0.6\textwidth}
      \begin{itemize}
        \justifying
        \item The majority of neutron dose equivalent is deposited by fast neutrons.
        \item Once neutrons strike the patient, they are rapidly thermalized and therefore show a sharp decrease in dose equivalent with increasing depth.
        \item The neutron percent depth dose equivalent (PDDE) decreases by \textcolor{blue}{approximately 20\% per centimeter penetration in tissue} \cite{kry_neutron_2009}, and because the spectrum does not change, these data are a good estimate of the out-of-field PDDE for any distance, field size, or SSD.
